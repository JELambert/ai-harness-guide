# Claim card: "Are coups good for democracy?"

Derpanopoulos, Frantz, Geddes and Wright (2016), Research & Politics 3(1), doi:10.1177/2053168016630837. Open access, CC BY-NC 4.0. Read from the HTML full text on journals.sagepub.com, which has no page numbers, so citations are to sections.

| Field | What the paper says | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main claim | Coups against dictators sometimes lead to democracy, but more often install new autocratic elites and bring more repression. | Abstract; Conclusion |
| Coup data | Powell and Thyne (2011) | Footnote 3; Background |
| Regime data | Geddes, Wright and Frantz (2014), updated | Footnotes 3 and 5 |
| Repression | Fariss (2014) latent human-rights scores, inverted so higher means more repression | Coups and repression |
| Event data | Worldwide Atrocities Dataset (1995-2014) and SCAD (1990-2013) | Coups and repression |
| Unit and sample | All autocratic regime-years in 285 dictatorships, 1950-2015; coups against democracies excluded | Coups and regime change |
| Model | Linear model with regime-case fixed effects, year fixed effects, regime and leader duration; separate pre-1990 and post-1989 coup terms | Coups and regime change |
| Key number | Regime change follows 68% of successful coups pre-1990 and 90% after | Introduction |
| Key number | New autocracies follow 56% of coups in the Cold War and 50% in 1990-2015 | Introduction |
| Key number | Democracy within two years follows 14% of successful coups 1950-1989 and 40% 1990-2015 | Background |
| Key number | 48% of attempted coups failed in the Cold War, 63% after | Coups and regime change |
| Key number | A successful coup raises the chance of a new autocracy by 19% (Cold War) and 27% (after) | Coups and regime change, Figure 1 |
| Event-data sample | 49 coup attempts after 1989: 30 failed, 7 democratizing, 7 adverse change, 5 reshuffling | Introduction; Figure 3 |
